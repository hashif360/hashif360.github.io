The long way to the keyboard

Born in India, raised in Jeddah, taught by a second-hand book on computers — the winding path from a UKG classroom to shipping software for a living.

  • life
  • origin-story

I was, technically, born in India. But my first real memories are of Saudi Arabia — so depending on how you count, I either come from a place I barely remember or a place I don’t remember arriving at. That small ambiguity has followed me around my whole life, and honestly, I’ve grown to like it. It meant home was never one fixed dot on a map. It was wherever the people I loved happened to be.

Two countries, one childhood

School started for me at Little Folks, where I turned up for UKG with the seriousness only a five-year-old can muster. From there it was a long, steady run at the International Indian School Jeddah — first standard all the way through the twelfth. Twelve years in the same place is a strange kind of luxury. You watch yourself change against a backdrop that mostly doesn’t. The same corridors, the same friends growing taller beside you, the same city outside the gate.

For most of those years I had no idea what I wanted to do. And then, around the eighth standard, something quietly rearranged my life.

The book that did it

Someone brought a book back from India — one of those SISO Books titles that set out to explain, in plain language, what a computer actually was. Not how to use one. What it was. How the pieces fit, why it did what it did, what was happening behind the screen.

I couldn’t put it down.

There was something about the idea that this machine wasn’t magic — that it followed rules, and that if you understood the rules, you could make it do things — that flipped a switch in me that has never switched back. I didn’t have a fancy setup or a mentor or a plan. I just had that book, a lot of curiosity, and an internet connection.

So I started building.

Learning by making

My first “websites” were held together on webs.com and weebly.com — drag-and-drop builders that let a teenager in Jeddah publish something to the actual internet. Looking back they were rough — and they’ve long since vanished from the web — but that wasn’t the point. The point was that I made a thing, and other people could open it. That feeling — this exists now because I made it — is the same feeling that still gets me out of bed to write code today. It hasn’t aged a day.

From websites I wandered into games, tinkering with 3DRad to build small, janky, wonderful little worlds. None of them were good. All of them taught me something. I was learning the only way that ever really stuck for me: by making things that didn’t work yet, and refusing to stop until they did.

India, entrance exams, and Chengannur

Then came the part every Indian kid raised abroad knows is coming: going back to India for the entrance exams and college. It’s a peculiar rite of passage — returning to a home country that you’re technically from but have to learn from scratch. New systems, new pace, a whole different kind of independence.

I landed at the College of Engineering Chengannur for a B.Tech in Computer Science. Finally, the thing I’d been teaching myself out of a second-hand book had a name and a curriculum. But the real education, as it turned out, still happened in the same place it always had — in the projects I built on the side.

CBatch and the joy of shipping

In my second year of college, I shipped something that actually mattered to people around me: CBatch, an Android app for my batch. It pulled together the boring-but-essential stuff — the syllabus, the timetable, previous years’ question papers — into one place students could just open. The APK still works if you want to poke at it.

Alongside it I kept making small games, the same way I always had — here’s one of them.

CBatch wasn’t going to change the world. But watching classmates actually use something I’d built to make their week a little easier — that closed a loop I’d first felt years earlier on webs.com. Build a thing. Put it in front of real people. Learn what happens next. It’s still, to me, the entire job.

The working years

After college, the tinkering became a career. I joined CareStack, got my first real taste of building software at scale and shipping to people who depended on it. From there I moved to DACGroup, where I still am — still building, still learning, still chasing that same feeling the SISO book handed me in the eighth standard.

Looking back

If you’d told that kid in Jeddah — the one hunched over a borrowed book, publishing clumsy websites to a free host — that it would turn into a whole life, he probably wouldn’t have believed you. He wasn’t thinking about a career. He was just thinking, how does this work, and what can I make it do?

Turns out that’s the only question I ever really needed. Everything since — two countries, a degree, an app, a job, and whatever comes next — has just been me, still asking it.
