Everything the résumé leaves out

The other story — happy beginnings, years of quiet fear, walks to tuition, a loss that broke a year, and the long work of finding my way back.

  • life
  • personal

I’ve already written the tidy version of my life — the one about books and code and shipping. This is the other one. The one underneath. Because a life isn’t really made of job titles and launches; it’s made of the years in between, the ones that don’t fit on a résumé but quietly decide who you become.

So here’s the rest of it, as honestly as I can tell it.

A happy start

I was, by every account, a happy kid. My earliest memories are soft and warm — playing with blocks on the floor, the particular pride of an A+ report card, the uncomplicated joy of a childhood where the biggest question was what to play next.

One of the first friends I ever made, Nidhin K Thomas, I met back in UKG. Together we sat for the entrance exam to get into the International Indian School Jeddah for the first standard — two small kids writing a test we barely understood, at the start of a road neither of us could see the end of. There was a lot of playing in those years, and a lot of learning, and for a while that was the whole world.

The years of quiet fear

Then, around the fifth or sixth standard, something happened that I understood only in fragments at the time.

My father got tangled in a court case at his workplace. The details were beyond a kid my age, but the feeling wasn’t. His exit had been stamped — and yet we were still in Saudi, unable to leave, and there was a real fear that if he was caught, he could be detained and deported. For years, going outside carried a low hum of dread. I was too young to grasp the legal machinery, but I understood fear perfectly. Children always do.

Looking back now, I think those years planted something in me — an anxiety that took a long time to name, let alone unlearn. But the story didn’t end there. He won the case. And out of all that pressure, he did something I still find remarkable: he started his own business. The fear finally lifted, and life opened back up.

Phones, walks, and a science stream

Somewhere around eighth or ninth standard, the world got bigger and louder — phones arrived, and Facebook, and suddenly there was a whole internet full of people.

My gadgets were gloriously humble. Hand-me-down phones passed through my hands — a Siemens A60, a Nokia 3230 — each one a small treasure. My first real phone came in the eleventh standard: a Chinese Anycool D9000, which, incredibly, had a built-in analog TV. I thought it was the most futuristic thing ever made.

I remember the walks to tuition with Manhar and Shahbas more vividly than most of the classes themselves. As tenth loomed, we’d walk and talk — about phones, about games, about whatever teenage boys decide is important that week. I took the science stream — chemistry, maths, physics — and by twelfth there was this electric mix of exam nerves and wide-open possibility. We were all making plans for a future we couldn’t wait to reach.

India, and the year everything broke

Then came the move to India — and with it, the quiet difficulty of fitting in somewhere you’re technically from but don’t quite belong to yet. Language barriers, new rhythms, the work of rebuilding a sense of home from scratch.

And then, in my final year, the ground gave way.

My sister — five years older than me — died in an accident.

There isn’t a clean way to write that sentence, so I won’t try to dress it up. Losing her in the middle of everything else was a grief I didn’t have the tools for. Some losses don’t get resolved; you just slowly learn to carry them, and to keep walking with the weight. I’m still learning.

And it wasn’t only my own grief I had to hold. My parents broke — completely, the way only parents who lose a child can break. I was still half a kid myself, and suddenly I was the one trying to hold up two people whose entire world had collapsed. I sat with them through it, listened to their sadness pour out in long, aching stories — about her, about life, about everything they were afraid of now. Those stories painted my picture of the world in colours I was far too young to be mixing. You grow up fast in a house like that. You learn what pain really costs, and how heavy love can be, and how to keep standing anyway because someone has to. I don’t think I’ve ever fully put that weight down. But I’ve made a kind of peace with carrying it.

Work, lockdown, and building my way back

Life, indifferent and merciful in equal measure, kept moving. I started working — first at CareStack, then at DAC — and found in the work something steadying. Then 2020 arrived with the COVID lockdown, and a whole new kind of fear settled over everything.

But that strange, suspended time also became, oddly, a turning point. Through the work-from-home years of 2020 to 2022, I poured myself into building Lesson21 — and, just as importantly, into the harder and less visible project of working on myself. The old anxieties, the grief, the trauma I’d been carrying since childhood — I finally started facing them head-on. By 2022, I’d come out the other side of a lot of it. Not fixed, exactly. But lighter. More whole.

I built through it all, and I never really stopped. A short-lived social platform for education called Study Chat. Interviewable, which I worked on from 2021 all the way to 2025. On a 2025 vacation I threw together SayWhen as a quick experiment — a strange little mashup of Calendly, Linktree and a kanban board, built mostly to see if I could. And in 2026 came DoosraBox, easily the most complicated thing I’ve built outside of work.

Underneath the named projects there was a constant, nameless churn of others — video-calling systems, WebRTC experiments, WebSocket servers, WhatsApp clones, more than I could ever list or even remember. The building was never separate from the healing; somehow they were the same act. Making things has always been how I make sense of being alive.

And yet — sometimes I look back at all that toil, the nights and the weekends and the endless half-finished experiments, and I honestly don’t know whether to feel proud or foolish. I see people who put in half the work I did land in far better places, and a quiet voice asks whether I got it all wrong, whether all that building was effort I should have spent on something easier or smarter. I don’t have a clean answer. Maybe there isn’t one. But I don’t think I could have lived any other way — and I’m slowly learning to let that be enough.

The shine in the desert

The truth is, the fire started early — long before any keyboard. As a kid I was forever “inventing” things, taking the world apart just to see what was inside. I blew out the house fuse more than once. I earned myself a proper electric shock or two along the way. None of it scared me off; if anything, those little jolts felt like the world talking back.

When software arrived, that same fire found a cleaner outlet — and it grew into something bigger. A quiet, almost embarrassing conviction that I was going to build something that mattered. That I’d be the next Steve Jobs, the next Bill Gates. That I was, somehow, special.

I’m old enough now to feel that certainty slowly winding down, the way a battery does. The dream is quieter than it once was. But it hasn’t gone out — there’s still a shine out there somewhere, a glint on the horizon like light coming off desert sand, and I still find myself walking toward it. And underneath all the grand ambition sits a plainer truth I’ve never doubted: I’m good at solving problems. I can usually find a way through — and often it’s an unconventional one, a door nobody else thought to try. That part has never dimmed. Maybe that’s the real fire. Maybe it always was.

Here, now

And that brings me to 2026, writing this down at last.

I’m not the anxious kid afraid of a knock at the door anymore. I’m not stuck in the worst year of my life. I carry my sister with me, and the fear, and the joy, and the blocks-on-the-floor happiness of the very beginning — all of it, at once. That’s what a life is, I think. Not a clean arc, but everything held together at the same time.

There’s more of it still to go. And for the first time in a long time, I’m genuinely looking forward to it.
