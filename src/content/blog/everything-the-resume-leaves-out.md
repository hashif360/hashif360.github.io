I've already written the tidy version of my life — [the one about books and code and
shipping](/blog/the-long-way-to-the-keyboard/). This is the other one. The one underneath.
Because a life isn't really made of job titles and launches; it's made of the years in between,
the ones that don't fit on a résumé but quietly decide who you become.

So here's the rest of it, as honestly as I can tell it.

## A happy start

I was, by every account, a happy kid. My earliest memories are soft and warm — playing with
blocks on the floor, the particular pride of an **A+ report card**, the uncomplicated joy of a
childhood where the biggest question was what to play next.

One of the first friends I ever made, **Nidhin K Thomas**, I met back in UKG. Together we sat
for the entrance exam to get into the **International Indian School Jeddah** for the first
standard — two small kids writing a test we barely understood, at the start of a road neither of
us could see the end of. There was a lot of playing in those years, and a lot of learning, and
for a while that was the whole world.

## The years of quiet fear

Then, around the fifth or sixth standard, something happened that I understood only in fragments
at the time.

My father got tangled in a court case at his workplace. The details were beyond a kid my age,
but the *feeling* wasn't. His exit had been stamped — and yet we were still in Saudi, unable to
leave, and there was a real fear that if he was caught, he could be detained and deported. For
years, going outside carried a low hum of dread. I was too young to grasp the legal machinery,
but I understood fear perfectly. Children always do.

Looking back now, I think those years planted something in me — an anxiety that took a long time
to name, let alone unlearn. But the story didn't end there. **He won the case.** And out of all
that pressure, he did something I still find remarkable: he started his own business. The fear
finally lifted, and life opened back up.

## Phones, walks, and a science stream

Somewhere around eighth or ninth standard, the world got bigger and louder — **phones** arrived,
and **Facebook**, and suddenly there was a whole internet full of people.

My gadgets were gloriously humble. Hand-me-down phones passed through my hands — a **Siemens
A60**, a **Nokia 3230** — each one a small treasure. My first *real* phone came in the eleventh
standard: a Chinese **Anycool D9000**, which, incredibly, had a built-in **analog TV**. I
thought it was the most futuristic thing ever made.

I remember the **walks to tuition** with **Manhar** and **Shahbas** more vividly than most of
the classes themselves. As tenth loomed, we'd walk and talk — about phones, about games, about
whatever teenage boys decide is important that week. I took the **science stream** — chemistry,
maths, physics — and by twelfth there was this electric mix of exam nerves and wide-open
possibility. We were all making plans for a future we couldn't wait to reach.

## India, and the year everything broke

Then came the move to **India** — and with it, the quiet difficulty of fitting in somewhere
you're technically *from* but don't quite belong to yet. Language barriers, new rhythms, the
work of rebuilding a sense of home from scratch.

And then, in my final year, the ground gave way.

My sister — **five years older than me** — died in an accident.

There isn't a clean way to write that sentence, so I won't try to dress it up. Losing her in the
middle of everything else was a grief I didn't have the tools for. Some losses don't get
resolved; you just slowly learn to carry them, and to keep walking with the weight. I'm still
learning.

## Work, lockdown, and building my way back

Life, indifferent and merciful in equal measure, kept moving. I started working — first at
**CareStack**, then at **DAC** — and found in the work something steadying. Then **2020** arrived
with the **COVID lockdown**, and a whole new kind of fear settled over everything.

But that strange, suspended time also became, oddly, a turning point. Through the work-from-home
years of **2020 to 2022**, I poured myself into building **[Lesson21](/about)** — and, just as
importantly, into the harder and less visible project of working on *myself*. The old anxieties,
the grief, the trauma I'd been carrying since childhood — I finally started facing them head-on.
By 2022, I'd come out the other side of a lot of it. Not fixed, exactly. But lighter. More whole.

I built through it all. A short-lived social platform for education called **Study Chat**.
**[Interviewable](/about)**, which I worked on from 2021 all the way to 2025. The building was
never separate from the healing — somehow they were the same act. Making things has always been
how I make sense of being alive.

## Here, now

And that brings me to **2026**, writing this down at last.

I'm not the anxious kid afraid of a knock at the door anymore. I'm not stuck in the worst year
of my life. I carry my sister with me, and the fear, and the joy, and the blocks-on-the-floor
happiness of the very beginning — all of it, at once. That's what a life is, I think. Not a clean
arc, but everything held together at the same time.

There's more of it still to go. And for the first time in a long time, I'm genuinely looking
forward to it.
